Why on-device

Cloud AI sends your words to someone else's servers.
This doesn't.

FAMTEC runs its language model directly on your phone's own chip — the same way Saftio's crisis detection stays local. Nothing about a normal conversation reaches a server, because there is no server for it to reach. The only network calls the app makes are the one-time model download and, if you explicitly approve it, a single web search.

Why this matters beyond one app

Your work shouldn't live inside someone else's subscription.

Every prompt sent to a cloud AI service passes through infrastructure you don't own, under terms you didn't write and can't fix in place. That vendor can raise its price once you depend on it, change what it retains, get acquired, or shut the product down outright — and the drafts, records, and reasoning you built inside it go with it, or stay behind on someone else's server. This is the same lock-in problem enterprises and governments are now treating as a serious risk in AI procurement: the practical test of dependency is whether a vendor outage, a policy change, or a price rise can take your working environment away from you. The 2026 shift toward open-weight models as owned infrastructure rather than rented access is the same argument applied to entire nations. FAMTEC applies it at the scale of one person's phone: the model is a file you hold, not a service you rent, and it keeps answering after the company that shipped it stops updating it.

Why free doesn't have to mean extractive

Free is only suspicious when the business model needs your behaviour.

Shoshana Zuboff's critique of surveillance capitalism is more precise than the old line that "if it's free, you're the product." Her point is that you're the source of the product: your activity is captured as raw material, refined into a behavioural asset, and sold onward — a relationship she calls extractive because it is not reciprocal. That model only makes sense when a company needs your data to run its actual business. FAMTEC's architecture removes that need at the root: there is no server receiving your conversations, so there is no pipeline for a behavioural product to be built from them, now or after a future acquisition, policy change, or new revenue push. This isn't a promise about intentions — it's a structural fact about where the code runs. It's the same reasoning behind the wider on-device AI movement — tools like Ollama, GPT4All and Jan on the desktop, and Apple's own framing of on-device processing as the reason your requests aren't "used to target ads or sell you something." FAMTEC takes that further than a hybrid device/cloud split: there is no cloud fallback for chat at all.

How it works
Step 1

Download a model, once

Choose Quick Intelligence (639 MB, works on devices with 3 GB+ RAM) or Everyday Intelligence (1.83 GB, 6 GB+ RAM). Both are official Qwen3 releases, pinned to an exact Hugging Face revision and verified with SHA-256 before the app will use them.

Step 2

Chat, fully offline

Once installed, inference runs entirely on-device via llama.cpp. Turn off Wi-Fi and it still works. Conversations stay in memory and clear when the app restarts — nothing is written to a database, local or remote.

Built-in safety

Saftio checks every message before the model sees it.

Saftio is a deterministic, on-device crisis-detection engine that runs locally, before inference — included by default, no download required. If a message reads as a crisis, the model is never called: the app responds immediately with sourced, verified Australian support resources instead. Saftio is a local pattern-based support feature, not a clinical service, and doesn't guarantee detecting every risk.

Optional web search

Off by default. Asks before every question.

When you turn on web search in Settings, FAMTEC asks — every single time — before sending your typed question to DuckDuckGo's keyless, no-tracking Instant Answer API. No API key, no account, no analytics SDK. Decline, and the model answers from what it already knows. Every other message in the app never leaves your device.

For developers

One downloaded model, built to power a family of sovereign apps — and to keep getting better.

Download the model once, and it becomes a shared resource: sibling apps — starting with Daily Grace, Aura, and Kings+ — read from the same on-device weights instead of each bundling their own multi-hundred-megabyte copy, the same way an operating system's shared libraries save every app from re-shipping the same code. Third-party integration is planned through a versioned request/result contract, App Intents and deep links, not by exposing the private model container directly. The economics work the opposite way to a subscription: one download, reused, is cheaper for everyone the more apps draw on it — not a recurring cost per app, per user, per month.

This also means the model on your phone is not fixed at the version you first downloaded. The three-line entry that names a model in this app — its source, its exact revision, its checksum — can point at a better release without asking anyone to pay for a new app or a new tier. As open-weight models improve and phone memory and neural-engine capacity grow, the same free app can carry a materially stronger model in twelve months than it does today, at no cost to whoever already installed it. Ownership compounds; a rented subscription does not.

Local-first software

Martin Kleppmann and colleagues at Ink & Switch set out seven ideals for software that keeps data private and secure "by architecture, not policy" — the standard this app is trying to meet, not just state. inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first

Open-weight models as owned infrastructure

The World Economic Forum's framing of open-weight AI as the difference between renting access and owning infrastructure — applied here at the scale of a phone, not a data centre. weforum.org

The Age of Surveillance Capitalism

Shoshana Zuboff's account of extractive, non-reciprocal data relationships — the precise version of "free" worth worrying about, and the one this architecture is built to make structurally impossible, not just policy-promised against. The Age of Surveillance Capitalism

The wider local-AI toolchain
